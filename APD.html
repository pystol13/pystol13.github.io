Ride Along

On the morning of April 16, 2007, Seung-Hui Cho entered Norris Hall at Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University in Blacksburg, Virginia carrying a backpack leaden with several chains, locks, a hammer, a knife, two handguns, nineteen magazines, and almost 400 rounds of ammunition. He came from West Ambler Johnston Hall, where he had just shot two fellow students dead. Once in Norris, he chained the doors shut and left a note saying a bomb would explode if the chains were removed. He then started firing and killed 30 people within ten minutes. As the police arrived, Cho committed suicide.

On the morning of April 16, 2007, Brian Matuszko woke up after a night patrolling the streets of Amherst, Massachusetts. He heard about what happened in Blacksburg, and shook with fear at the implications. Virginia Tech is a public university very similar in size and population to the University of Massachusetts – Amherst. Both have student populations measured in the tens of thousands, both are flagship land-grant state universities. Both have sprawling campuses that are difficult to keep secure.

Cho's killing spree hit Matuszko close to home. He regularly patrols in and around the UMASS campus, at least passing through several times a shift. Due to mutual assistance agreements between the UMASS Police Department and the Amherst Police Department, Matuszko knows that if a shooting similar to Virginia Tech occurs, he will be one of the first responders. He is afraid of the possibilities. He is a sane human being, he has no hero complex. He says, “I'm not out to save the world, I'm out to do my job." He doesn't want to run headlong towards a shooter holed up inside an academic building. Matuszko knows that if there is a massacre at UMASS, the people he is trained and sworn to protect, the students and citizens of Amherst, MA, could become targets that he may have to shoot. In a way, he has to protect us from ourselves.

Since 9-11, the Federal government has mandated more training from many governmental employees. The idea is to get first responders, mainly cops and firemen, informed so that in the event of a catastrophic attack, they can get a situation under control until federal authorities arrive. Recently Matuszko went to New Mexico for a course called “Incident response to terrorist attacks.” The Department of Homeland Security funded it entirely. He was excited, “We went around the room, and everyone introduced themselves, and said a little blurb about why they were there. Virtually everyone said, 'I'm just here to blow shit up'.”

While everyone there appreciated the fun of government sanctioned explosions, the real reason they blew various things up in the New Mexican desert was to then investigate what occurred, how it worked. Matuszko said, “We blew up a car and several small buildings. We were taught by Homeland Security just how much crucial evidence remains at the scene and what to look for during post blast investigations.”

What Brian wasn't expecting to take with him was the day to day application of the knowledge he gained from this course. Now when he sees a Pioneer Valley Transit Authority bus loaded to the gills with reveling students on a Friday or Saturday night, he shudders. Pointing from his patrol car, he says, “If someone were to put a pipe bomb on that bus, there would be absolutely no survivors. None. That scares me to death.”

Hearing Brian Matuszko say he is scared of something is a very strange experience. This is because of his stature. Matuszko is at least six foot four without his uniform boots on. Those add another two inches. His erect spine keeps him at his full height all shift. Weighing in at 275 pounds, he is built like a linebacker, even though his position was usually tight end. He attributes at least some of his size to “getting into protein shakes while in high school.” Now 28, he has been taking dietary supplements for more than ten years. It shows.

Off duty, his neutral expression is relaxed, languid. Tired, almost. But while on patrol he is alert and attentive. Driving his patrol car down North Pleasant Street, past bars and coffee shops and houses, his head is on a swivel, constantly observing his surroundings. This can make him both tense and terse at times, and he doesn't appreciate it when people aren't straightforward. He raises his voice, yelling at an imaginary person while we drive down South Pleasant Street, “Don’t waste your and my time with lies. Just be honest with me, and you’ll find I’m a pretty easy going guy.” Lying makes him irritated, he readily admits this, and it is obvious from his reaction to the mere thought of dishonesty. As he gets irritated his eyebrows raise higher and higher. When he is really angry, his blue eyes are wide open and his eyebrows are halfway to his closely cropped gelled hairline, and his smooth boyish face is suddenly menacing. By this point, Brian is staring unblinking bullets into the eyes of the suspected offender. I only saw him get like this when someone lied to him.

On a Thursday night in late fall around ten o‘clock, Brian decides to keep an eye on a popular local liquor store, the Spirit Haus. It is misting rain and chilly, but warm and foggy inside the patrol car. Matuszko parks across the street from the store and turns off the headlights. He is in full view of the parking lot and the doors to the Spirit Haus, but it's one of his favorite places to stake out for liquor law violations. If one person goes in the store while the driver stays in the car while it’s running, you have to ask why that person doesn't go inside as well, Matuszko says. As he says this, a Ford Explorer is doing just that, headlights still on. Four shadowy heads stay in the car while a fifth from the back seat gets out and runs inside. He comes out minutes later with three thirty racks of cheap beer, and throws them in the back. He opens the door to hop in. Brian has been on stake out for no more than ten minutes.

In one motion Matuszko drops the car into gear and flicks on the headlights. He guns the car across the street and stops behind the Explorer, blocking them from backing out. He gets out and strides right to the driver's window. "Where's the beer fellas?" he asks. No response. "Huh guys? Where's that beer you just bought?" The driver says there isn't any beer in the car. Matuszko's eyebrows go up a notch. He shines his Mag-Lite in the back window, onto two cases of Keystone Light and one of Busch Light.

"You're lying to me. Whose beer is that? Let's see some IDs." The driver says it all belongs to the sole twenty one year old in the car. All ninety beers. Brian's eyes widen more.

"Your story, for lack of a better term, is B.S." he says tersely. He says "BS" like it is a curse word itself. The IP (police jargon for involved person) keeps babbling, trying to keep his story straight. He claims he's the only one with a car, and that the beer isn't his, and that he isn't lying. He has good reason to be nervous and conciliatory. Possession of alcohol by an under aged person is a criminal offense.

Matuszko cuts the driver off. "You made a mistake. I caught you. Deal with it." More excuses and pleas. Brian shines the flashlight in the IP's face, and repeats, louder; "I just caught you doing something illegal. Deal with it."

He takes the purchaser out behind his cruiser, and takes down his information. Brian's demeanor is calmer now, he is quietly talking with the guy who bought the beer.

The driver and everyone in the car is underage, but the man standing with Matuszko is twenty one and the purchaser. He readily admits to buying beer for these minors. Because of his cooperation, Brian decides not to arrest him on the spot. “I'm not arresting you, but I have to take down your information. It's not my call at this point, you may or may not receive a summons in the mail.” He then turns towards the remaining people in the car, and speaks with the driver again. He gives a similar, but slightly harsher, speech. He takes the beer and loads it into his patrol car.

As he pulls out of the liquor store parking lot Matuszko deadpans to me, "Just to prove that we don't take this out in the woods and have a good time, I'm going to show you how we process evidence."

The "paperwork" room is big and square. There are desks running down the left and right sides of the room. There are about half a dozen computers spread out along the desks. Dozens of intraoffice mailboxes line the walls. There is a poster opposite the door as you walk in, the Monadnock Baton Chart. In green, yellow, and red it shows the critical places to hit a human being with a baton, and the probable consequences of hitting them in that area. Green means little to no permanent damage, places like the arms and legs. Yellow is possible permanent damage, but those are also the soft spots that will subdue a person immediately, like collar bones, kneecaps, and the groin. The poster instructs to use force here if the subject is actively resisting the police officer. Red areas are the spine, head, solar plexus, and neck. Trauma there will "tend to range from serious to long lasting rather than temporary and may include unconsciousness, serious bodily injury, shock or death."

A desktop printer screeches to life and spits out three adhesive bar codes. They are for the confiscated beer, so they can be identified in the evidence locker. Matuszko has finished his narrative describing the liquor law violation. "LLVs", in cop talk. He puts the beer in "sarge's shopping cart", kept in a small extra room off the paper work area. The Amherst Police Department confiscates so much alcohol on a weekend night that they need a shopping cart to get it from the paperwork room where it is entered into the computer system, down the hall to the evidence room.

Brian joined the APD in 2003. "I'm not going to lie, give you some cliche. I wanted to be a cop because I like blue lights and going fast... I've wanted to be a police officer my whole life.” He never considered applying to other law enforcement agencies, he is happy working in Amherst. He likes the slow summers, and the small town feel. He sees himself as a people person, a problem solver. "If I can fix a problem for even a month, a week, a day, an hour, I feel like I've done my job."

Before becoming a cop, Matuszko spent seven months at a classified location in western Iraq as a member of the Air National Guard. He was an aircraft fuel systems mechanic at a special forces base from February through July 2003. He worked on A-10s, the military's flying tank busters. In his spare time, he played football with the other Americans at the base. Soldiers stationed there from other coalition countries, Poles and Brits among others, would drink tea and watch, marveling at this bizarre American past time.

While stationed in Iraq, Matuszko would visit the BX, or Base Exchange, regularly. He called it “pretty much three aisles of a Wal-Mart, and you have to stand in line for an hour just to get in.” It was one of the few things to do in the Iraqi desert. One time he was standing in line, and the person in front of him was none other than Young, a teammate of Matuszko's from the Norwich University football team. They had lost touch over the years, now they were reunited in Iraq. Young was now LT Young of the US Marines.

Attending Norwich, even for only one year, had a profound effect on Brian. He was a Core Cadet, and though he says that he didn't like it, “It helped shape me into the person I am today... It was a trip but I'm glad I did it... my mom was confused when I came home from break and would make my bed immaculately and shine my shoes... I had been a normal eighteen year old before... that discipline has stayed with me to today.”

Amherst police officers estimate that during the school year, about 70% of calls they respond to are student related. Much of this is “nuisance crime”, such as drunk and disorderly conduct, noise complaints, and petty vandalism. It ties up a lot of man hours, something the department does not have to spare. Due to budget cuts, the Amherst police have transitioned entirely to what Chief Charles Scherpa calls a “reactive only department.” By this he means that there are no more programs that get cops out in the community for the purpose of preventing crime. As it is, they only respond to crime. Chief Scherpa said this makes for a very negative view of police. “The only time citizens see police is if something bad has happened. We only respond after the fact. Because of this, people come to associate us with negative things.”

Some of the programs Scherpa had to cut were cops in schools, DARE, the mounted unit, juvenile diversion, and a ropes course the police ran in the Notch, a state park in South Amherst. He liked these programs because they prevented crime before it occurred. “You put a mounted unit downtown, people come look at it, kids pat the horse, residents interact with the officers in a situation that is not stressful or negative. You don't get that putting a patrol car or even a motorcycle downtown. People don't react the same way.” He thinks it helps put a human face on police officers. When asked which program he would bring back first if he had the funds, the Chief immediately answered, “the mounted unit.”

Now, since being forced to whittle down his budget program by program, Scherpa faces challenges. “I have a responsibility to keep this community safe, no matter what the cost. It all depends on what the town wants.” He says, “Officers have less time to compromise. They don't have as much time to be courteous, we have more to do with less.” The title of the Amherst Police Department Annual Report for 2007 was titled, “Trying to Do the Best with Less.”

At 11:30 on an unseasonably warm Friday night, a call came over the radio about a noise complaint at 798 North Pleasant Street, not 100 yards from the UMASS campus. The dispatcher said that the APD has visited that house 4-5 times since the beginning of the semester, all for noise complaints. As the radio crackles Matuszko mutters to himself, "Looks like someone might make the team." I don’t understand his comment at the time, but I realize later that an arrest is likely. As proven by the previous four complaints within the past two months, this is a house whose residents do not respond to verbal warnings.

Matuszko turns off his blues and parks the patrol car on an adjacent street and walks up to the house with two other officers who have already arrived. They trudge up the muddy, puddle ridden driveway to the main entrance, on the side porch of the house. There are flags of various countries covering the windows, all you can see are silhouettes when someone walks in front of a light. Two young men stand on the porch smoking cigarettes. One asks the officers as they arrive, "Did you get a noise complaint?" Brian curtly responds, "No, we're just here to hang out." The man says he is a criminology student at UMASS. He is wearing a baggy gray sweat suit. He wants to talk to the officers about their jobs and how it relates to his major. They are not interested. The men won't leave until Matuszko threatens to arrest them for Failure to Disperse. Meanwhile the officers pound on the door. As the door opens, Matuszko slips his foot in the jam to keep it from closing all the way. A man answers the door, he is probably a student. He does not live there. "Find someone who lives here, then leave." No residents come to the door. "If you don't live here, leave," the officers keep shouting into the house.

After conferring with the other two officers present, Matuszko walks around the front to the other side of the house. The other two cops will stand by the front door to try and find someone that lives there. As Brian walks around to the other side of the house, he is greeted by a sea of party goers, all hiding where they thought the police wouldn't check. He walks right through the middle, his Mag-Lite cutting a path through the crowd. "If you don't live here, go home," he announces to no one in particular as he walks towards the back door. There are at least 75 party goers milling around in the darkness. There is garbage everywhere, abandoned Solo cups, cardboard from empty cases of beer, and numerous unidentifiable objects. A broken lawn chair rests upside down. A party tent is set up on the left, by the tree line.

Matuszko comes up behind a man wearing a white baseball cap sitting in a wicker chair by the door. He has a can of Bud Light in his hand. "Do you live here?" Matuszko asks. The student says yes without looking, then sees who is asking the question, and immediately changes his mind. "No, no I don't." Matuszko repeats, "Do you live here?" "No." "Why did you just say yes? Why are you lying to me?" He berates the student for not being honest, grills him for information on the party and who lives there, then tells him to go home. He yells in through the door, telling everyone to go home. He then has a young man in an Ellis Hobbes jersey lock the door.

Brian walks around the front of the house again. The officers speak with the only resident present, the student in the Patriots jersey. He is also wearing basketball shorts and white sneakers. His two roommates that helped host the party had fled when the police arrived. The student is arrested and driven downtown for booking.

Most of the party goers are still standing in front of the house, some of them in the middle of the street. They are completely disregarding traffic. It’s obvious what they are doing, they are waiting until the police leave so they can resume partying at the house. They aimlessly wander, talking, flirting, laughing.

Sergeant Sullivan arrives on the scene and tells the loiterers to move along, the party is over. He uses a quieter tone with the remaining partiers than the initial responding officers. He has gray hair, and is significantly older than the other officers. Perhaps it is because of this reason that the students comply. Two of the responding officers are only a few years older than the students. If it weren't for the badge and gun, they could easily resemble an older cousin, brother or friend.

Close to midnight on a Thursday night Matuszko and I are sitting on a small side street, right where a parade of students pass on their way to Frat row and the bars downtown. Matuszko likes to park here with his headlights off, he says it's only a matter of time before someone starts slugging a beer or taking a leak right in front of him. From the quiet radio, we listen to the Patriots fold to the Jets. The radio also provides the only light to see by, which isn’t much. While we sit, eyes slowly growing accustomed to the darkness, we talk, and the conversation drifts to the role of police in society. “We provide a valuable service to the community, absolutely. But we don't produce anything... we're a big drain on the town.”

He makes a good point. The diminishing budget of the Amherst Police Department has far reaching effects on the day to day activities of the officers on patrol. While we sit waiting to respond to another noise complaint, or to a drunken fist fight, or to interrupt a student looking to relieve himself on the sidewalk, some disturbed, reclusive Seung-Hui Cho imitator is sitting alone in his dorm room, plotting to make violent headlines. Maybe if more resources were available to spend on preventing crime, there would be less nuisance offenses, and the police could focus and ensure that the next Cho won’t have a chance to pull off his twisted stunt. No one can say with absolute certainty. In any event, as it stands now, Matuszko spends his shifts making sure that the noise is kept under control, that the drunk student doesn‘t pee on the Smiths shrubs, and telling people to go home when the party’s over.
